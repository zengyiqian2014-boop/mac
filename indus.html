I. The Indus Valley Civilisation

Urban Planning and Thermodynamic Efficiency

The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) achieved an urban planning precision that was not replicated until the Industrial Revolution. Their cities, including Mohenjo-Daro and Harappa, were built upon a rigid, cardinal grid system that fundamentally altered the thermodynamic profile of the urban environment.

Passive Cooling and Wind Corridors

The cardinal grid was not purely for aesthetic order; it was a deliberate climate-responsive engineering strategy. By orienting major arteries according to the prevailing wind directions, Harappan engineers created natural "wind tunnels." These streets acted as convective pathways that flushed out heat generated by high-density residential occupation. As air flowed through these narrower streets, the Venturi effect accelerated air speed, effectively cooling the micro-environment.

Structural Thermal Lag

The Harappans pioneered the use of kiln-fired bricks with a standard 1:2:4 ratio. This mass-produced, standardized material provided incredible thermal inertia. During the day, the thick, heat-absorbent walls of the residential structures absorbed incident solar radiation. This delayed the heat transfer into the interior space (thermal lag). By the time the heat would have penetrated the interior, the sun had set, and the cooler night air began to cool the walls. This natural cycle maintained stable indoor temperatures without energy consumption.

Sanitation and Public Welfare

While often overlooked, the Indus sanitation system was the most advanced in the ancient world. Their sophisticated drainage system featured covered sewers, manholes, and soak pits to prevent contamination. This focus on public health enabled high-density living without the common plagues that devastated other contemporary civilisations.
