I. The Indus Valley Civilisation

A Case Study in Passive Thermodynamics and Urban Heat Mitigation.

The Grid as a Thermodynamic Machine

The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) represents an early pinnacle of systematic urban planning. Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro were not developed organically; they were engineered with a rigid cardinal grid structure. This was not merely for aesthetic organization, but a sophisticated response to the harsh regional climate. By strictly aligning street networks, planners intentionally created convective pathways for regional wind currents.

Fluid Dynamics: Harnessing the Venturi Effect

The streets functioned as Passive Wind Corridors. By optimizing street width in proportion to residential building heights, Harappan engineers utilized the Venturi Effect, where wind velocity increases as it moves through constricted spaces. This effectively accelerated cool air circulation, flushing out trapped heat and reducing the ambient urban temperature without any active mechanical energy consumption.

Thermal Mass and Material Integrity

The universal adoption of the 1:2:4 brick ratio across the civilization was a triumph of material standardization. These kiln-fired bricks possessed high Thermal Inertia. Throughout the day, the thick masonry absorbed solar radiation, delaying heat penetration into the living quarters (a process known as Thermal Lag). By the time the stored heat would have reached the interior, the external temperature had dropped, allowing the walls to discharge heat back into the cooler night air, maintaining a remarkably stable indoor microclimate.

Engineering Output

Maintained indoor temperatures significantly below external peaks through structural thermodynamics.
